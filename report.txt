This report describes our project focusing on finetuning small language models to generate valid
Scalable Vector Graphic (SVG) images given
a description of an icon/symbol for a Kaggle
competition. SVGs are eXtensible Markup Language (XML) based, human-readable, and scalable.
SVGs require valid syntax and structure, making
this task much harder than generic text generation. Another challenge is that the typical models
are trained on text, not graphics, meaning most
small models will not have a great understanding
of SVGs.
Our approach was to fine-tune a pre-trained
model on a dataset of descriptions and SVG pairs
using Low Rank Adaptation (LoRA). This allowed
for efficient fine-tuning without updating all of the
model’s weights. Submissions are scored against
ground-truth images using a pre-provided similarity metric. Our best model achieved a strong score
on the public leaderboard, outperforming around
two-thirds of the class.

Inference and Post-Processing

Inference was performed by first loading the finetuned LoRA adapter on top of the quantized base
model. Generation followed with random sampling, a temperature of 0.2, and a top p of 0.9.
Batched inference with a size of 6 was used as
well.
After the model completed generation, the SVGs
were extracted using regex. A repair pipeline was
also applied, stripping invalid HTML tags in the
output. Also, if no valid SVG could be output, a
placeholder SVG would be submitted in its place
(white background with black circle).

The training was done using a competition-given
dataset (NYU Tandon Deep Learning Course Staff,
2026b) consisting of 50,000 samples of description
and SVG pairs. The descriptions were in natural
language. We chose Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (Qwen
Team, 2024) as our base model. It was a solid
choice as it is pre-trained on tasks such as coding
and following instructions. It was also only 3 billion parameters (the competition maximum was 4
billion).

LoRA Rank and Alpha

The second experiment used a LoRA rank of 8 and
an alpha of 16, while the third experiment used
16 and 32, respectively. A higher rank means the
adapter can represent more complex weight updates. We wanted to give the model more capacity
to learn SVG geometry. However, our results contradicted this expectation as the third experiment
scored slightly lower, even though we expected
much improvement (that model was also trained for
much longer). A possible reason for this could be
that the higher rank could have caused the adapter
to memorize specific path coordinate patterns from
the training SVGs, reducing generalization performance.

Experiments

Three main experiments were run. The first experiment used Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct. This model was
only trained for 7 steps (1 epoch) and was simply
used to confirm that the notebook was working.
The second experiment used Qwen2.5-3BInstruct, which was trained for 3,063 steps. This
model includes checkpoints to save the training,
and it recorded consistent improvement in the evaluation loss. This loss was consistent and showed
no signs of overfitting. The result from this experiment was also our final submission, achieving
a score of 15.03. This run also recorded a 35.1
percent fallback rate, mostly due to truncation of
outputs. It is important to note that we did attempt
another inference run with this model with different
parameters; however, the score dropped to 14.40.
The third and final experiment, double training
capacity by increasing LoRA rank from 8 to 16
and alpha from 16 to 32. This experiment trained
for 5090 steps and consistently reduced loss, showing no signs of overfitting. Even though all signs
were positive during training, the leaderboard score
came out to 14.81, which was slightly worse than
the previous run. We also saw a 34.1 percent fallback rate.
We did try increasing the max new tokens from
2000 to 3500, but we still saw around a 30 percent fallback rate. We were forced to end this run
midway through due to time constraints.

4.3

Training Duration

Experiment 2 was trained for 1 epoch (around 3
thousand steps), while experiment 3 was trained
for 2 epochs (over 5 thousand steps). Experiment
2’s loss progression consistently decreased every
500 steps, slightly slowing down between 2500 and
3000 steps. Experiment 3 also showed a consistent
decrease in loss, but there was a slight plateau after
2500 steps, indicating that the second epoch was
not necessary.
4.4

Inference Decoding Strategy

The second experiment used a greedy decoding
with no repetition penalty. During the second run
of that same experiment, a repetition penalty of 1.3,
a temperature of 0.2, and a top p of 0.9 were all set.
This decreased the accuracy, reducing the score
to 14.40. For the third experiment, we reverted to
greedy decoding and lowered the repetition penalty
to 1.05.

4.5

Maximum Sequence Length

The second experiment used a maximum sequence
length of 1024, which meant that around 20 percent
of the training SVGs would be truncated. Because
of this, we increased it to 2048 for the third experiment; however, this caused an out-of-memory
crash on our GPU. We ultimately settled on 1536,
which covers 88 percent of the training data. Despite this improved coverage, we still saw a lower
score.

System Prompt

The system prompt for the second experiment was
"You generate compact, valid SVG markup from
user requests. Return only SVG code with a single
root <svg> element." In the third experiment, it
was changed to "Prefer simple shapes (rect, circle,
polygon, line, path) with minimal coordinates."
The motivation for the change was to try to reduce
the sequence length, steering the model toward
simpler outputs.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the three comparable 3B runs.
The 1.5B pilot is omitted because it only trained for
7 steps and was used only to verify that the pipeline
worked end-to-end. The best public leaderboard
score was 15.03 from the first 3B adapter. The same
trained adapter dropped to 14.40 when we changed
only the decoding strategy at inference time, while
the higher-capacity v2 adapter achieved a lower
validation loss but a slightly worse leaderboard
score of 14.81. The loss curves in our analysis
notebook show that both 3B runs trained stably and
that v2 consistently achieved lower held-out loss
than v1.
The fallback rates remained high in both main
runs: 35.1 percent for v1 and 34.1 percent for v2.
This means that relatively small changes in validity
and truncation behavior had a large effect on the
final metric.

Observations

Several patterns stand out. First, the best result
came from the simplest inference strategy: greedy
decoding with no repetition penalty. SVG generation is much more brittle than ordinary text generation because minor perturbations to numeric
coordinates or path commands can break validity.
The sampled run with a repetition penalty of 1.3
likely hurt performance because the penalty distorted repeated numeric and path tokens that are
actually normal in SVG syntax.
Second, the lower held-out cross-entropy loss
did not translate into a better leaderboard score.
The v2 model achieved the best eval loss but still
underperformed the simpler v1 run on the public
leaderboard. This is consistent with the competition metric: once an output becomes invalid, its
3

score is zero, and even valid outputs are judged
by rendered similarity rather than token likelihood.
Standard language-model loss was therefore useful
during training, but it was not a sufficient proxy for
SVG validity and visual fidelity.
Finally, the debug logs show that many failures
were not semantically wrong icons so much as
structurally broken outputs. Common issues included generations that stopped in the middle of a
long path command, invalid XML attributes, hallucinated HTML or prose inserted into the SVG,
and occasional coordinate drift far outside the intended canvas. These observations suggest that
future gains are more likely to come from structured decoding and stronger validity control than
from simply training longer.

Conclusion

References

LoRA fine-tuning of a 3B instruction model was a
viable approach for this competition and produced
a competitive result under modest hardware. The
strongest submission did not come from the largest
or longest-trained configuration; instead, it came
from the cleaner one-epoch adapter paired with
conservative greedy decoding. This suggests that,
for text-to-SVG generation, the reliability of syntax
and renderability mattered more than squeezing out
additional improvements in token-level loss.
If we continued this project, we would focus on
methods that directly target validity: length-aware
training to reduce truncation, constrained or XMLaware decoding, and a stronger reranking or repair
stage for candidate SVGs. We would also isolate
variables more carefully in future ablations. Our v2
experiment changed LoRA rank, alpha, sequence
length, system prompt, and training duration at the
same time, which made it harder to attribute the
final score drop to any single factor.
The main limitation was compute. All training
was done on a single RTX 3080, sequence lengths
above 1536 caused out-of-memory failures, and
both training and full-dataset inference were slow
enough that only a small number of end-to-end submission runs were feasible. We also had practical
issues such as notebook resets, long inference jobs,
and the need to resume partial runs from checkpoints. We even tried training on an M1 MacBook
Pro for a time, but training took too long. Colab
was proving troublesome since our runtime would
often be interrupted. Those constraints limited how

Representative Failure Modes

Inspection of the debug logs for both main runs
revealed three recurring failure types. The most
common was truncation: the model often produced
a plausible opening SVG block and then stopped
in the middle of a long path command, leaving
the XML unclosed. A second failure type was
malformed markup, including invalid attributes,
mismatched tags, and occasional HTML or prose
fragments mixed into the output. A third type was
geometric drift, where the SVG remained partly
parseable but coordinates exploded outside the intended 256 × 256 canvas. These failure modes
explain why even small decoding changes could
materially affect the final score.
